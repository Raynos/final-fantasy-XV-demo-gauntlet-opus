**The ask:** build an action RPG in ThreeJS at the level of Final Fantasy XV
(PS4, 2016). Utterly perfect, AAA quality, everything procedural. Fan out
subagents, loop each item, have a harsh critic compare blind against the real
game. Never pigeonhole, always work the biggest lever.

## Phase 0 — Foundation (22:13 → 22:30)

I started by building the thing that would determine everything after it: a
**capture harness**. Before a single visual system existed, `tools/shoot.mjs`
could boot vite, wait for `window.GAME.ready`, step the simulation with a fixed
timestep for determinism, apply a named shot, write a PNG, and **exit non-zero on
any console error**. That last detail mattered more than I expected — it meant an
agent could never mistake a blank canvas for success.

I also decided the file-ownership model here: agents work in **git worktrees**
owning **disjoint directories**, and anything cross-boundary gets *reported*, not
edited. Across 115 commits this produced exactly one merge conflict.

First capture was a grey capsule on a green plane. Real Metal GPU acceleration in
headless Chromium, not swiftshader — which made the whole shoot-look-fix loop
affordable.

**Judgement call:** the README said "never use workflows, favor subagents", while
the harness told me the `ultracode` keyword opts into the Workflow tool. I
followed the user's explicit instruction over the default. Right call — the work
was far too interactive for a scripted fan-out.

## Phase 1 — The first wave (22:30 → 01:00)

Eight agents in parallel: atmosphere, terrain, vegetation, characters, combat,
UI, post-processing, and a pure-logic RPG systems layer.

They came back strong. The atmosphere agent built Rayleigh/Mie/ozone scattering
with GPU-baked LUTs shared between sky, fog, cloud lighting *and* IBL. Terrain
did 420k-droplet hydraulic erosion feeding a 7-level clipmap. The post agent
found and fixed waste I'd left in — GTAO was re-rendering the entire scene for
depth.

**First real lesson, and it was mine.** I merged the RPG layer, verified it
*booted*, and moved on. Much later a design agent found that all **5,765 lines of
it were dead code** — instantiated, ticked, and read by nothing, while the HUD
drew hardcoded literals over a complete simulation. I had even "fixed" a HUD
roster bug in the *mock data table* without noticing the HUD wasn't reading the
real model at all.

That single miss is the origin of `tools/integration.mjs`.

## Phase 2 — Merge integration (01:00 → 02:00)

Merging seven parallel branches is where the interesting bugs live, because each
agent was individually correct and the *combination* was not.

- Night blew out to white: `Sky` drove `renderer.toneMappingExposure` while
  `PostFX` ran its own auto-exposure. Two owners of one value, multiplying to 21.6×.
- Backlit bushes became opaque black boxes.
- The midday zenith went black.

I dispatched a dedicated integration agent, and it corrected two of my three
hypotheses. `MaterialPatch` was innocent; the "bushes" were actually `grass_far`,
and the cause was that `buildAlphaMips` quantised coverage to 0/1 below 8×8 and
flooded the coarsest mips opaque. The black zenith was an un-normalised dual-lobe
HG phase swinging ~350:1.

**Lesson: my diagnoses were plausible and wrong. The measurement was right.**

## Phase 3 — Critics (02:00 → 03:00)

Three harsh critics on the 12-shot contact sheet. Verdict: **3.5/10 — "a
competent tech demo, not a game."** Characters 2/10, world dressing 2/10.

They earned their keep by catching what I'd stopped seeing:

- A **data bug visible in the frame** — the HUD showed PROMPTO twice and
  GLADIOLUS as the level-27 leader. `Party.members` holds only the three
  companions, but the HUD merged them by index starting at Noctis's slot.
- "Four of twelve frames are 30% mud" — a foreground mound eating the bottom
  third of every gameplay shot.
- "The environment quality actively hurts you — it makes the characters look
  like placeholder capsules someone forgot to replace."

The most useful critic output wasn't a complaint, it was a *diagnosis*: the
mountain strata implied 30–50 m per bed, and **that** was why ranges read as "big
but near" rather than distant. Far better than "mountains look wrong".

## Phase 4 — The agents start disagreeing with the critics (03:00 → 05:00)

This is where the loop started producing better results than any single voice.

- A critic gave precise pixel measurements and said **Ignis's rig was the correct
  reference** to propagate. The character agent built a geometry-space measuring
  harness instead of trusting it, and found Ignis was the *worst* proportioned of
  the four at 8.39 heads — he'd only looked right because he was small and turned
  in frame. Propagating him would have made everything worse.
- A critic said there was "no hair geometry". There was. The real cause was that
  the face map had **no contrast-preserving mip chain**, so at 4–8 m the lash
  line, socket and brow box-filtered into flat skin — and the sclera sat at 0.27
  albedo, identical to the socket, so everyone read as squinting.
- The terrain agent was asked to tighten strata frequency. It found **three bugs
  suppressing the banding entirely** — the profile had its three modulators
  *multiplied* into it, steepness used `1 - N.y` (0.13 at 30°, excluding every
  real badland face), and the form-following term scaled by absolute world
  position. Tightening the frequency alone would have done nothing.

**Lesson: brief agents with the critique, not as orders but as evidence to test.**

## Phase 5 — The user starts playing (05:00 → 07:00)

The single highest-value intervention of the session was the user pushing back on
my measurements:

> "Fixing a little bit of FPS on a corpus of 5 posed shots won't fix the FPS of
> the game."

Completely right. I built `tools/gameplay.mjs`, which drives the real loop with
synthetic input across 13 segments. It immediately found what posed shots
structurally *could not*:

- **Weapon swap froze for 15.8 seconds.** Three separate freezes over 3 s.
- 755 ms stall crossing the map, 466 ms on weather change.
- 773 hitches in one session.

The root cause was beautiful: **toggling a light's `visible` changes three.js's
program key**, and recompiled 43 programs — a measured 9.5 s. Three systems were
doing it. Posed shots never call `setWeapon`, so they could never have caught it.

The user also caught that I was burning the machine: 40 chromium processes,
load average 18. Pointed me at a sibling project's harness, which documented that
**`--disable-frame-rate-limit` is a pure loss** — 1.74 cores → 0.58 idle, frames
byte-identical. I had it in both perf tools.

## Phase 6 — Content, and the honest reckoning (07:00 → 08:00)

The user asked for the gameplay content plan, and a design agent delivered the
audit that reframed the project: the RPG layer was dead, `Director.setScenario`
was "a photo booth, not an encounter system", and nothing in the game was
pressable.

Ten agents went out across the whole game: the RPG wire, Hammerhead with NPCs and
shops, a drivable Regalia, encounters and bosses, dungeons, story and cutscenes,
audio, the 8 km world map, wall-clock/load time.

The results were strong — 8192 m world with 124 POIs and a road graph verified
drivable by assertion; a Regalia with a saturating tyre model whose own test
caught three bugs including the car *floating 48 m down a cliff*; an audio system
verified by rendering 60 s through an `OfflineAudioContext` and asserting on the
buffer.

Then the user played it, and found what none of my tooling had:

> "you just clip through the campsite lol instead of going up the stairs"

**There was no collision. At all.** My integration audit tested that features
were *reachable*, never that the world was *solid*. A whole category I hadn't
thought to check.

Also: Escape fought the browser's pointer lock, menu entries were dead, entering
the car wasn't discoverable, the world map was "ghetto", and the clouds were
"ghetto" — which turned out to be an **undefined mip level inside divergent
raymarch control flow**, making the hardware pick the coarsest mip over large
regions: one uniform coverage value, no holes, saturated to black. That was the
slab across the sky.

## Phase 7 — Closing the loop (08:00 → 09:16)

Collision landed with 20/20 assertions (haven steps lift you 7.08 → 9.78 m).
Menus, pointer lock and the keymap landed with 86/86. The combat loop landed with
30/30 driven by real DOM events — and found that **`Director.play()` sets
`scenario='live'`, which the HUD didn't recognise, so the entire combat HUD layer
had never appeared in an unscripted fight.** Every combat HUD screenshot we had
came from posed scenarios.

Integration audit now reads **18 pass, 0 wired, 0 failed**. Perf went from a mean
of 17.5 fps to ~87 posed. Terrain init 8,320 ms → 285 ms via a build-time bake.
Single-shot capture 23.6 s → 1.5 s warm.

## What I got wrong

- **Verified boot, not integration.** The dead RPG layer sat there for hours.
- **Dispatched two agents onto `terrain/**`** and had to tell one mid-flight that
  the ground had been rewritten under it.
- **Broke the build with a scripted edit** (a doubled comma in `Shots.js`) — dev
  had already parsed the module so only `vite build` caught it, and the harness
  hung for 120 s with no useful error. The user suggested the pre-commit hook.
- **Reported a bug as verified when my check was invalid.** I "confirmed" a
  `heightAt` mismatch by raycasting, forgetting the terrain displaces in the
  *vertex shader*, so I was hitting the undisplaced lattice. I corrected it
  publicly; it remains unverified.
- **My first integration audit reported six failures that were all my probe
  guessing wrong APIs.** I introspected the real interfaces and fixed the probe
  rather than reporting false failures — but I nearly shipped the false report.
- **Let quality agents add cost while perf was failing.** Props added +7.2%
  triangles at 17 fps.

## What worked

- The harness before the game.
- Disjoint worktree ownership: one conflict in 115 commits.
- Telling agents to look at their own screenshots, ≥5 rounds.
- Critics that grade against the real game, never against improvement.
- Treating agent reports as claims: two agents disproved critics by measuring,
  one disproved another agent's 8.9 ms figure (real answer 0.3–1.2 ms) and
  correctly left the code alone.
- The user playing the build. Every one of the highest-value findings this
  session — gameplay perf, collision, dead menus, the clouds — came from a human
  actually using it, not from any automated check I wrote.
